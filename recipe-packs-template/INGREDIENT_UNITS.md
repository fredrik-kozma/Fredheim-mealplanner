# Ingredient units — house style

The rule the recipe collection follows, worked out (and corrected) while
converting ~450 existing ingredient rows to be consistent:

> **Solids are measured by weight (grams). Liquids, powders and spices are
> measured by volume (dl/ml/l) or spoon (tsp/tbsp).** A handful of solids
> stay countable because a weight would be actively worse.

Use this when writing a new recipe, and when reviewing a pasted one before
it goes into a pack.

---

## 1. The decision

For each ingredient, ask in order:

1. **Is it a liquid?** (water, plant milk, oil, juice, broth, vinegar,
   tamari/soy sauce, extract, syrup, honey, maple syrup) → **volume**
   (dl/ml/l for a bulk amount, tbsp/tsp for a small one). Never grams.

2. **Is it a powder or a spice/seasoning?** (nutritional yeast, psyllium/
   fibre husk, salt, ground spices, baking powder/soda, thickening starches
   like cornstarch or tapioca) → **tsp/tbsp**. Never grams — "3 g cumin" is
   worse than "1 tsp cumin", not more precise in any way that matters at the
   stove. Two exceptions the collection made on purpose: **flour** and
   **thick pantry pastes** (tomato purée, peanut butter, tahini, soy yogurt,
   soy mayonnaise, sour cream, date paste) went to **grams** even though
   they're powder-or-paste-like, because they were the worst offenders for
   inconsistency (flour alone showed up as `400 g`, `1.2 dl` and `4 l` in
   different recipes) and a kitchen scale is genuinely how people measure
   them.

3. **Is it a solid whole food** — vegetable, fruit, nut, seed, legume,
   grain, dried fruit? → **grams**, regardless of whether the recipe would
   naturally reach for a `dl` measure, a spoon, or a count (`1 onion`). See
   the density and per-piece tables below.

4. **Exceptions that stay countable** — a weight would make the recipe
   *worse*, not more precise:
   - **Garlic** → cloves (`clove` unit). Cooks think in cloves, "fedd"
     reads naturally in Norwegian, and half a clove is still an actionable
     instruction.
   - **Lemons / limes / oranges** → pieces (`pcs`). Almost every use is
     "juice of 1 lemon" or "zest of 1 lemon" — the fruit itself is never
     weighed, so a gram figure would describe the wrong thing.
   - **Nori sheets, dry-yeast envelopes** → the natural discrete unit
     (`pcs`, `envelope`) — these are sold and used as one whole unit at a
     time.
   - **Bay leaves and pinch/dash seasonings** (salt, cayenne, nutmeg,
     turmeric, smoked paprika, vanilla powder) → see §4, the "to taste"
     convention, rather than any numeric unit.

## 4. The "to taste" convention

For ingredients where a scaled number is actively meaningless — bay leaves
(0.2 g each, fished out before serving), a pinch of salt, a dash of cayenne.
**Do not** invent a gram figure or leave `quantity: 0`. Instead:

```json
{ "quantity": null, "unit": "", "name": "Sea salt, to taste" }
```

- `quantity: null` (not `0` — see §6 on why `0` is wrong)
- `unit: ""`
- the amount folded into the **name**, once, in each language:
  - EN: `, to taste` — or `(1–2 leaves)` for bay leaves
  - NO: `, etter smak` — or `(1–2 blader)`
  - SV: `, efter smak` — or `(1–2 blad)`

This is the pattern the fasting-plan pack already used for salt before any
of this cleanup — it's the house convention, not a new invention.

## 5. Rounding

- Below 20 g: round to the nearest whole gram.
- 20 g and above: round to the nearest 5 g. Nobody weighs 58 g of cashews;
  60 g is the right amount of precision.

## 6. Never do this

- **`quantity: 0` with an empty unit.** This was the single most common
  data bug found: it displays no amount at all and contributes nothing to
  the shopping list, silently. If there's truly no measurable amount, use
  the §4 "to taste" convention (`quantity: null`). If there *is* an amount,
  estimate it from the recipe's own method (see how many git-history
  examples: `scripts/fill_missing_amounts.cjs`) — never leave it at zero.
- **A quantity with no unit at all** (e.g. `quantity: 0.5, unit: ""` on a
  countable item). Always pair a quantity with a real unit.
- **Converting dried herbs, whole spices, or thickening starches to
  grams.** Basil *dried* stays `tsp`; basil *raw/fresh* converts to grams.
  Cornstarch and tapioca starch stay `tbsp` even though they're
  technically powders measured like solids — a cook reaches for a spoon,
  not a scale, for a thickener.
- **Guessing a novel ingredient's density.** Check a USDA cup-weight (or
  the equivalent reference for your food-composition source) and divide by
  2.4 for g/dl. Four ingredients in the original pass were off by 20–44%
  from memory alone (quinoa, bulgur, canned beans, arugula) — cross-check
  before trusting a number you wrote from memory.

## 7. If you're editing existing recipes with a script

Three language arrays (`ingredients`, `translations.no.ingredients`,
`translations.sv.ingredients`) must move together. Before any bulk edit:

1. **Verify the arrays are the same length** for every recipe, in every
   language present.
2. **Verify each index agrees on quantity and unit** across languages
   (unit spelling differs — `tbsp`/`ss`/`msk` — but the *value* must match).
3. Only then rewrite by index, in all three arrays at once, in the same
   script pass.
4. **Bump the pack's `version`** in its JSON file. `AutoInstallDefaultPack`
   only reinstalls a pack when the version string changes — editing recipe
   content under an unchanged version means it never reaches anyone who
   already has the pack installed.
5. Re-run the same two checks *after* writing, to catch a bug in the script
   itself before it ships.
